\section*{Overview}

This document presents the exploratory data analysis (EDA) conducted as part of 2034 in the wine quality prediction project. The goal is to investigate how properties — including alcohol and residual sugar — of wines relate to quality in both red and white wines. This task analysis will guide future predictive modelling.

\section*{Task 1A: Initial Data Analysis}

\subsection*{Quality Distribution – Red Wine}

The first analysis focused on the distribution of the \texttt{quality} variable in the red wine dataset. The values range from 3 to 8, with most samples concentrated around 5 and 6.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../figures/red_wine_quality.png}
    \caption{Distribution of Quality Scores in Red Wine Dataset}
    \label{fig:red_quality}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:} The majority of red wine samples are rated 5 or 6 in quality. Higher scores (7 and 8) occur less frequently, and very few samples are rated below 4.

\subsection*{Quality Distribution – White Wine}

The same distribution analysis was applied to the white wine dataset.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../figures/white_wine_quality.png}
    \caption{Distribution of Quality Scores in White Wine Dataset}
    \label{fig:white_quality}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:} Like red wine, white wine samples mostly fall in the 5–6 quality range. However, white wine has a wider spread, with a noticeable number of samples rated 7 or 8, and more rated 4 as well.

\subsection*{Red vs White Comparison}

To compare the two datasets, the quality distributions were plotted side-by-side.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/wine_quality_comparison.png}
    \caption{Side-by-Side Distribution of Red and White Wine Quality}
    \label{fig:combined_quality}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red wine quality scores are tightly clustered around 5 and 6.
    \item White wine exhibits more diversity, with higher and lower quality ratings more common.
    \item This variation may be influenced by grape types, fermentation techniques, or sample size (white wine has over three times more records).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Task 1B: Alcohol Content and Wine Quality}

\subsection*{Overview}

This task examines whether alcohol content is associated with perceived quality. Alcohol levels were categorised as \texttt{low}, \texttt{mid}, or \texttt{high} based on the dataset's mean and standard deviation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{low}: below (mean – standard deviation)
    \item \texttt{mid}: within one standard deviation of the mean
    \item \texttt{high}: above (mean + standard deviation)
\end{itemize}

The new variable \texttt{alcohol\_cat} enables comparison of quality across these groups.

\subsection*{Red Wine}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/red_quality_by_alcohol.png}
    \caption{Red Wine Quality by Alcohol Category}
    \label{fig:red_quality_alcohol}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red wines with higher alcohol levels tend to have higher quality scores.
    \item There is a clear upward shift in the distribution of quality from \texttt{low} to \texttt{high} alcohol groups.
    \item Low-alcohol red wines are rarely rated above 6.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{White Wine}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/white_quality_by_alcohol.png}
    \caption{White Wine Quality by Alcohol Category}
    \label{fig:white_quality_alcohol}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A similar, though less pronounced, trend is observed in white wine.
    \item Higher alcohol white wines are rated slightly better, on average.
    \item The distribution is narrower than in red wines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Interpretation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alcohol content is positively associated with wine quality for both types.
    \item This may be due to alcohol signalling better fermentation or grape maturity.
    \item \texttt{alcohol} appears to be a strong candidate for predictive modelling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Task 1C: Residual Sugar and Sweetness Classification}

\subsection*{Overview}

This task explores whether residual sugar affects perceived quality. A new binary feature \texttt{isSweet} was created by splitting each dataset at the median residual sugar level:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{isSweet} = 1 if residual sugar $\geq$ median
    \item \texttt{isSweet} = 0 otherwise
\end{itemize}

This provides a roughly balanced classification of wines into “dry” and “sweet”.

\subsection*{Red Wine}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/red_quality_by_sugar.png}
    \caption{Red Wine Quality by Sweetness (\texttt{isSweet})}
    \label{fig:red_quality_sweet}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sweet red wines (\texttt{isSweet}=1) score slightly lower on average.
    \item Quality distributions for dry and sweet red wines overlap substantially.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{White Wine}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/white_quality_by_sugar.png}
    \caption{White Wine Quality by Sweetness (\texttt{isSweet})}
    \label{fig:white_quality_sweet}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item White wine shows a clearer quality distinction.
    \item Dry wines tend to have higher scores than sweet ones.
    \item Sweet white wines are more frequently rated 5 or lower.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Interpretation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Residual sugar has a weak negative correlation with quality.
    \item This suggests that drier wines may be perceived as higher quality, possibly due to taste expectations.
    \item \texttt{isSweet} could be a useful feature in predictive models, though its influence is less than alcohol.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Task 1D: Quality vs Sweetness}

This task evaluates whether sweet wines are perceived as higher or lower quality, using the binary \texttt{isSweet} feature created in Task 1C.

\textbf{Red Wine:}  
The distributions for dry and sweet red wines are very similar. Sweet red wines show a slightly lower median quality score, but the overall shift is minimal.

\textbf{White Wine:}  
White wines show a clearer separation between dry and sweet. Dry white wines tend to score higher, while sweet white wines are more concentrated in the lower range (mostly 5 or below).

\textbf{Conclusion:}  
Sweetness appears to **negatively impact** perceived quality for white wines, but **has minimal effect** in reds. The \texttt{isSweet} feature may therefore contribute more to prediction in white wine modelling.

\section*{Task 2: Correlation Analysis}

To identify which variables might be most useful for predicting wine quality, correlation matrices were produced for both red and white wine datasets. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used as it measures **linear relationships** between continuous features. This is appropriate for the physicochemical variables provided.

\subsection*{Red Wine Correlation Matrix}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/red_wine_matrix.png}
    \caption{Correlation Matrix for Red Wine (Pearson)}
    \label{fig:red_corr}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Key Findings:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol} shows the strongest positive correlation with quality ($r \approx 0.48$).
    \item \textbf{Volatile acidity} is moderately negatively correlated with quality.
    \item \textbf{Sulphates} and \textbf{citric acid} show weak-to-moderate positive correlations.
    \item \textbf{Free sulphur dioxide} and \textbf{total sulphur dioxide} are highly correlated with each other (redundant).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{White Wine Correlation Matrix}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/white_wine_matrix.png}
    \caption{Correlation Matrix for White Wine (Pearson)}
    \label{fig:white_corr}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Key Findings:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol} again has the strongest positive correlation with quality ($r \approx 0.44$).
    \item \textbf{Residual sugar} and \textbf{density} are weakly negatively correlated with quality.
    \item Sulphur dioxide variables remain strongly intercorrelated, which may introduce multicollinearity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Interpretation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol} is a consistently strong feature and will likely be a key predictor in modelling.
    \item \textbf{Volatile acidity} and \textbf{density} may serve as negative predictors.
    \item Highly correlated variable pairs (e.g., \texttt{free\_SO2} and \texttt{total\_SO2}) may be reduced to avoid redundancy.
    \item This correlation analysis will inform feature selection for the machine learning models in Task 3.
\end{itemize}
